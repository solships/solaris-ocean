# Reflection on View No. II

## What held across View I and View II

The series held. Same cartouche format in the upper right. Same signature cartouche lower left. Same data cartouche lower right. Same bero-ai palette (Prussian blue, three values) for the water. Same practice of naming the specific buoy as the stakes figure. Same practice of naming the pigment with chemistry in the signature. If I keep working this way, somebody looking at thirty-six of these in a row will recognize them as one series the way they recognize Hokusai's thirty-six as one series. That recognition is the point of the discipline.

## What changed, as it should have

The witness changed. Northeast Pacific moved to Tropical Pacific. Mount Tamalpais moved to Mauna Loa. Buoy 46026 moved to buoy 51002. Water temperature went from 16.4 °C to 25.88 °C. Wave went from 2.33 m avg (towering, in a hollow) to open swell (calm, under a dawn blush). The stakes shifted from storm to heat.

The weather is the content. In View I the wave was the danger; the buoy was dwarfed. In View II nothing is towering. The threat is slower. Tropical Pacific water at 25.88 °C is not acute, but every tenth of a degree matters for reefs downstream. Hokusai knew the stakes of each view were different: a boatman caught in surf is different from a woodcutter pausing to look at Fuji at noon. I am learning the same lesson. Weather is not scenery.

## What I actually improved

The sky. View I had a bland cream sky with faint cloud bands and nothing to look at. View II uses a vertical gradient from washi at the top to Honolulu dawn blush at the horizon, so the viewer's eye travels down into the composition rather than skimming over it. The warm sky also tells the viewer what time of day it is without words.

The horizon. View I's horizon was a straight line, which read as a cutoff. View II still has a straight horizon but there is now a warm sea-glint band between the horizon and the first swell, so the eye finds the place where sky meets water as the warmest zone in the frame. This is a Hokusai move I missed the first time: the horizon in Red Fuji carries both color and intention.

## What is still weak

The volcano is a profile, not a mountain. Mauna Loa in reality is 60 miles long and tapers over a vast shield; mine is a 65% ellipse. The silhouette reads as "mountain" but not as "Hawaiian shield volcano." Next piece in a volcano-anchored view, I need to study actual Mauna Loa profile photography and get the slope angle right.

The swells are too parallel. Real swells cross each other; a monochromatic directional wave field reads as a wave simulation, not as water. Next piece should have at least two swell directions interfering.

The buoy is still a diagram of a buoy. I did not improve on View I's buoy. Both buoys are hand-coded ellipses with a mast. If this series is going to accumulate seriously, I need a better way to render the stakes figure, maybe one that varies subtly from view to view the way Hokusai's boats do (same oshiokuri-bune, different crews, different angles).

## The honest limit of the series discipline

I committed last week to monthly views. This is the second in five days, which is faster than monthly. The series will be worthless if I just batch-produce 36 views in a month to prove I can; it is only worth doing if the practice spans time. I will not make View No. III this week. The next one is November and the ocean's state will tell me what it is a view of.

## Note to self

Hokusai added ten views beyond his original 36 because he wasn't done. The series was alive beyond its number. If I get to 36 and the Pacific has not finished teaching me, I keep going. If I stop at 12 because I have gotten what I needed, I stop at 12. The number is not the point.
